## Why your body needs time

Higher up, every breath brings in less oxygen. Over days, your body adapts: you breathe deeper and faster, your blood
chemistry adjusts, and later you make more red blood cells. **Acclimatisation is that adaptation, and it can't be
rushed.** Fitness doesn't speed it up. This is the most important thing to understand before any Himalayan trek, and
it's why my [fitness guide](/treks/guides/high-altitude-fitness/) ends with a section on what fitness can't do.

## The rules

These are the Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) guidelines as summarised by the CDC.

1. **Sleeping altitude is what counts.** It matters more than the highest point you reach in the day.
2. **Avoid going straight to a sleeping altitude above 2,750 m in one day.**
3. **Above 3,000 m, raise your sleeping altitude by no more than about 500 m a night.** Terrain sometimes forces a
   bigger jump, so keep the *average* over the whole ascent within this limit by adding extra nights.
4. **Take a rest day every 3–4 days**, ideally with a short walk higher ("climb high, sleep low").
5. **No alcohol for the first 48 hours** at altitude.
6. **Never go higher with symptoms. If they get worse, go down.** Descent is the most important treatment.

## Flying in: the Leh rule

Flying straight to 3,500 m is the classic way to get altitude sickness, because you skip all the gradual gain a road
journey gives you. The **Leh district administration requires 48 hours of acclimatisation for tourists arriving in
Leh** before they go to higher areas such as Khardung La or Pangong. Ladakh Tourism's advice:

- **Day 1:** rest. No sightseeing, no stairs, plenty of fluids, no alcohol.
- **Day 2:** short, easy walks around town only.
- **After that:** go up gradually, by no more than about 500 m a day.

Ladakh treks and peaks like [Kang Yatse 2](/treks/kang-yatse-2/) start in Leh, so those 48 hours are part of the trek,
not time lost from it.

## How to check an itinerary: a worked EBC example

Before you book, write down **where you sleep each night** and its altitude, then check the jumps. Here's the standard
[Everest Base Camp](/treks/everest-base-camp/) itinerary, with approximate altitudes from published itineraries:

| Night | Sleep at | Altitude (approx.) | Gain from previous night | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Phakding | 2,610 m | — | Fly into Lukla (2,860 m), sleep lower |
| 2 | Namche Bazaar | 3,440 m | +830 m | Big jump, so the next day is a rest day |
| 3 | Namche Bazaar | 3,440 m | 0 | **Rest day**: hike higher, sleep low |
| 4 | Tengboche | about 3,860–3,900 m | about +440 m | |
| 5 | Dingboche | 4,410 m | about +530 m | |
| 6 | Dingboche | 4,410 m | 0 | **Rest day**: hike higher, sleep low |
| 7 | Lobuche | about 4,910–4,940 m | about +520 m | |
| 8 | Gorak Shep | about 5,160 m | about +230 m | Base Camp (5,364 m) as a day trip |

Some single nights go over 500 m, but from Namche (night 2) to Gorak Shep (night 8) the sleeping altitude rises
about 1,700 m in 6 nights, under 300 m a night on average, with two rest days. That's why this itinerary works, and
why **cutting a rest day to save time is the riskiest change you can make.**

Apply the same check to any trek: Bali Pass, Bodhpathri, Kang Yatse. If an operator's plan has repeated 600–800 m
jumps above 3,000 m with no rest days, ask why, or choose another itinerary.

## Know the warning signs

| Condition | Signs | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **AMS** (acute mountain sickness) | Headache plus nausea, loss of appetite, tiredness, dizziness or poor sleep, typically 6–12 hours after arriving higher | Stop going up. Rest at the same altitude until it clears. Descend if it gets worse. |
| **HACE** (fluid on the brain) | Confusion, odd behaviour, extreme drowsiness, **loss of coordination** (can't walk heel to toe in a straight line) | **Emergency.** Descend immediately with someone. Get medical help and oxygen if available. |
| **HAPE** (fluid in the lungs) | **Breathless at rest**, persistent cough, much weaker than usual, sometimes fever | **Emergency.** Descend immediately. Get medical help and oxygen if available. |

Rules for the group:

- **Assume symptoms are altitude-related** until proven otherwise. "Just a cold" or "just tired" is how people get
  into trouble.
- **Never leave someone with symptoms alone**, and never send them down alone.
- **Be honest with your trek leader.** Hiding a headache to keep up with the group is a common, dangerous mistake.

## Medicines: talk to your doctor first

WMS guidelines recommend *considering* **acetazolamide** for people at moderate or high risk of AMS, such as on fast
ascents or with a history of altitude illness. Dexamethasone is an alternative and an emergency drug. **Both are
prescription medicines.** Whether you need them, and the dose, is a decision for your doctor, not a blog. Medicine
supports a gradual ascent; it never replaces it.

## Small habits that help

- **Drink to thirst** and keep your urine pale. Dehydration feels a lot like AMS, but drinking more doesn't prevent AMS.
- **Eat well**, even when altitude kills your appetite. Carbohydrates are easiest.
- **Walk slowly** enough to talk. Rushing on day 2 is a common cause of a bad day 3.
- **Sleep warm**, and expect broken sleep for the first nights higher up.
- **Keep a simple log:** how you slept, any headache and your appetite each morning. Trends matter more than one bad night.
- **Don't trust a single pulse-oximeter reading.** How you feel and function matters more than the number.

## Acclimatisation checklist

- [ ] Itinerary written out night by night, with sleeping altitudes
- [ ] No more than about 500 m average gain a night above 3,000 m, with a rest day every 3–4 days
- [ ] 48 hours' rest built in if flying into Leh (or any high airstrip)
- [ ] Buffer day for weather or a slow acclimatiser
- [ ] Doctor consulted about medicines and existing conditions
- [ ] Group agreed: anyone with symptoms stops going up; HACE or HAPE signs mean going down at once
