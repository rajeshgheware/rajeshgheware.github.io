## What a trek like Bali Pass asks of your body

[Bali Pass](/treks/bali-pass/) (about 4,950 m) is roughly 60 km over 7–9 days, climbing steadily up the Tons valley to Ruinsara Tal and Odari. Then
comes a pre-dawn summit push, a steep climb to the pass and a long descent over boulders to Yamunotri. Indiahikes rates it
*Difficult* and says it's not for first-time trekkers. Physically, that means four things:

1. **Aerobic endurance:** walking 6–10 hours a day, several days in a row, with less oxygen than you're used to.
2. **Climbing endurance:** your legs and lungs working uphill for hours while you carry a pack.
3. **Strength:** legs, hips, core and lower back that keep you stable on steep, uneven ground and on the descent.
4. **Durability:** recovering overnight in a cold tent and doing it again the next morning.

Train all four. Being fit in only one of them, say a fast 10 km runner with weak legs on descents, is how people end up
struggling on the hardest day.

## Benchmarks: are you ready to start building?

Before trek-specific training, you should be able to do roughly the following:

| Test | Target | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Run 5 km | about 35–40 min (40–45 min if you're over 40) | Indiahikes general benchmark |
| Run 5 km / 10 km (Bali Pass level) | about 30 min / about 70 min | Trek operator requirement |
| Climb stairs non-stop | 100 floors with a light daypack | My own benchmark |

If you're well short of these, add 6–8 weeks of plain base building at the start.

## The 16-week plan

This follows the structure most mountain coaches use: a big easy aerobic base, two strength sessions a week, and long
days that build slowly. A typical week has **4 aerobic days, 2 strength days and 1 full rest day**. "Easy" means you can
hold a conversation. Most of your hours should feel like this.

| Phase | Weeks | Aerobic (easy) | Climbing-specific | Strength | Key session |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** | 1–6 | 4 × 45–75 min walk, jog or cycle; 5–7 h/week | 1 stair session, 30 → 60 floors | 2 × full body | One 2–3 h walk at the weekend |
| **Build** | 7–12 | 6–9 h/week | 2 stair sessions, building to 100 floors non-stop; pack 5 → 8 kg | 2 × (heavier, fewer reps) | 3–5 h hike or stair day with a pack |
| **Peak** | 13–15 | 8–10 h/week | Back-to-back long days at weekends with trek-weight pack | 2 × maintenance | **Test day: about 1,000 m of climbing in one go** |
| **Taper** | 16 | Volume down by about half, keep a little pace | 1 short stair session | 1 light session | Sleep, eat and pack |

If you have more time, do what I did: stretch the base phase over several months.

## Stair climbing: the city trekker's mountain

I had no hills nearby, so the stairs of my apartment block were my mountain. I logged every session:

| Month (2021) | Floors climbed | Sessions | Biggest session |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | 1,069 | 12 | 208 floors |
| February | 1,000 | 13 | 100 floors |
| March | 1,000 | 13 | 100 floors |
| April | 1,000 | 12 | 100 floors |
| May | 1,000 | 10 | 100 floors |
| June | 824 | 7 | 224 floors |
| **Total** | **5,893** | **67** | |

Then in **August 2021, a few weeks before the trek, I climbed 320 floors in 4 hours.** At roughly 3 m per floor that's
close to 1,000 m of ascent, and it told me my legs were ready for summit day.

What I learned:

- **Steady beats heroic.** Hitting 1,000 floors every month did more than any single big session.
- **Go non-stop.** Almost all my sessions were non-stop. That builds the rhythm you need for long climbs at altitude.
- **Log it.** A simple spreadsheet (date, floors, non-stop yes/no) kept me consistent for eight months.
- **Train the descent too.** Walk some sets down instead of taking the lift. Long descents, like the boulder field below
  Bali Pass, are where tired quads and knees give up.
- **Add a pack gradually** in the build phase, up to the load you'll carry on the trek.

## Strength: legs, core and lower back

Twice a week, about 45 minutes. Start light and add load slowly. Good form matters more than weight.

| Area | Exercises | Sets × reps |
|---|---|---|
| Legs (climbing) | Step-ups onto a knee-height box (add a pack or vest), walking lunges | 3 × 10–12 each leg |
| Legs (descending) | Slow step-downs (3 seconds down), split squats | 3 × 8–10 each leg |
| Hips and back of legs | Romanian deadlifts, glute bridges | 3 × 10 |
| Calves and ankles | Single-leg calf raises, balance on one leg | 3 × 15 each |
| Core | Planks, side planks, dead bugs | 3 × 30–60 s |
| Lower back | Bird-dogs, back extensions, suitcase carries | 3 × 10 / 30 m each side |

**Why I take core and lower back seriously:** on [Kang Yatse 2](/treks/kang-yatse-2/) (6,250 m) in September 2025,
severe lower back pain and a lack of physical energy on summit day made me turn back before the top. A strong engine
isn't enough. The structure carrying the pack has to last the full summit day.

## Pack training and boots

- Train with **the pack and boots you'll trek in**, so problems turn up at home rather than on day three.
- Increase the pack weight by 1–2 kg at a time, up to your trek load: typically 6–10 kg for a daypack, more if you won't
  offload your rucksack.
- Do your long days on uneven ground at least sometimes, such as trails, rocky hills or local treks, not only on stairs.

## Summit-day energy

Summit days start before dawn and can run 10–12 hours. Running out of energy can end the day as surely as poor fitness
can.

- **Practise eating on your long training days:** small amounts every 45–60 minutes, so you know what your stomach
  tolerates while climbing.
- **Drink steadily**, and carry water you can reach without stopping.
- **Eat a proper dinner and breakfast** before summit day, even when altitude kills your appetite.
- **Start the trek rested.** The taper week isn't the time to make up for missed training.

## Altitude: what fitness can't do

Being fit makes each day easier, but **it doesn't protect you from altitude sickness.** What protects you is how fast
your sleeping altitude rises. The Wilderness Medical Society guidelines (summarised by the CDC) advise:

- Avoid going straight to a sleeping altitude of **2,750 m or more** in one day.
- Once above **3,000 m**, raise your sleeping altitude by **no more than about 500 m a night**, with extra rest days if
  you climb faster.
- **Avoid alcohol for the first 48 hours** at altitude.
- If symptoms get worse (severe headache, vomiting, breathlessness at rest, confusion or loss of coordination),
  **go down.** Descent is the most important treatment.

Pick a trek itinerary that respects these rules, and never push on with worsening symptoms because you feel fit.
For the full picture, including how to check an itinerary night by night and the warning signs of HACE and HAPE, read my
[acclimatisation guide](/treks/guides/acclimatisation/).

## The last two weeks

- **Cut volume by about half and keep a little pace.** Short, brisk stair sessions keep you sharp.
- **No new exercises.** This isn't the time for soreness or niggles.
- **Sleep and eat well,** and get any blisters or aches looked at now.
- **Pack early**, then do one final short walk in your full kit.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Medical check-up done
- [ ] 5 km run benchmark met
- [ ] 100 floors non-stop with a daypack
- [ ] At least one about-1,000 m climbing day with a trek-weight pack
- [ ] Two consecutive long days completed
- [ ] Boots broken in over at least 50 km
- [ ] Summit-day food tested on training days
- [ ] Itinerary checked against acclimatisation rules
